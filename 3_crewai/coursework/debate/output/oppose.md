I oppose the motion.

Strict laws to regulate LLMs would be a mistake because they would freeze innovation, entrench big tech, and punish responsible developers more than harmful actors. The biggest danger is not that LLMs exist — it is that overregulation would make them too expensive and difficult to build safely, leaving the field to a few giant corporations and foreign competitors who can afford the compliance burden.

LLMs are general-purpose tools, like the internet or smartphones. We do not solve misuse by strangling the technology itself. We solve misuse by targeting harmful behavior: fraud, impersonation, privacy violations, and defamation are already illegal. The answer is enforcement of existing laws, not broad new restrictions that treat every model as a threat.

Strict laws would also slow beneficial uses in education, medicine, accessibility, and productivity. Smaller startups, researchers, and open-source projects would be the first to suffer, while large companies with legal teams would dominate the market. That reduces competition, increases dependence on a few platforms, and ultimately gives the public less choice and less transparency.

Finally, rigid regulation is too slow for a fast-moving technology. By the time a law is written, the models and risks have changed. Flexible, case-by-case oversight, industry standards, and targeted accountability are far better than blanket strict laws.

So the real choice is not safety versus chaos. It is smart governance versus heavy-handed control. We should regulate harmful uses, not impose strict laws that choke innovation and consolidate power.
